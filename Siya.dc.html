✦ 01 · The context

Three hours to write one question paper.

Teachers were assembling papers by hand: hunting through textbooks and past papers, balancing marks, matching the board's pattern, retyping all of it. Three hours, every cycle, all year.

The first siya was a straight line, nine steps and six screens for one user. It worked. Then we found MagicSchool and the question got harder: why would a teacher pick this one? The answer was the thing we already owned, an edtech repository with every board, publisher and grade in it.

✦ 04 · The product

The screens stay behind the door.

siya is live. Teachers in India and Malta are marking real answer sheets on these screens this week, and the product is the company's advantage rather than a portfolio asset. So the screens stay locked.

Reason two · the one that actually decides it

The buyer kept changing. Teacher, then school, then group, and each move pivoted the product underneath it. siya is B2B, so no two schools run the same siya: dashboards, reports and permissions are configured to how that school already works, and the feature backlog is long and still moving.

✦ 05 · The hardest screen

Designing for a machine that is sometimes wrong.

A teacher hands over a scanned answer sheet in a child's handwriting and gets marks back. The model is confident and occasionally wrong, and a wrong mark on a report card is not a rounding error, it is a parent in the principal's office. So the interface was built around the override, not around the score.

Four states carry the whole argument. The sequence is the work.

✦ 06 · The logo

Three elements became one, and it took an argument.

The product launched with an all caps wordmark, an atom mark and a tagline underneath. Nobody had asked for a brand system, and for months nobody wanted one.

Act one · the ask Branding can wait.

Keep shipping product, the identity comes later. For months, it did.

Act two · what broke A deck of cards that refused to stack.

Every brochure, business card and deck looked like it came from a different company. Nothing was holding them together.

Act three · what I did Built it anyway, then argued for it.

A lavender led palette, a lowercase chalkboard wordmark, a companion mascot. The first mascot got laughed at in the room. It shipped.

Before · the mark

An atom-style mark built from interlaced loops.

Before · the lockup

Caps wordmark, atom mark, tagline, three elements in one signature.

After · the wordmark

One element. Lowercase, rounded, lavender.

The four decisions inside that change
Caps to lowercase The same mark has to be trusted by a principal and liked by a nine year old. Caps read institutional. Lowercase, with rounded terminals borrowed from the mascot's glasses, reads like a companion.
The atom had to go An atom promises a laboratory. siya reads handwriting and returns judgement. Three elements in one signature also meant no version of it survived a small size.
One signature, five surfaces Product sidebar, browser tab, print header, hoarding, hoodie. One weight, one colour, one fixed position, so nobody downstream has a decision left to make.
The old mark still lives Schools already had it in their bookmarks bar, so the atom stayed on as the favicon. Continuity for the people already using the product, a clean slate everywhere else.
✦ 07 · The brand

A deck of cards that finally stacked.

For months the answer to “can we do branding now?” was “branding can wait.” Meanwhile every brochure and business card looked like it came from a different company, because nothing held them together. When I got the go-ahead, I built the system before the artwork.

The 70 / 20 / 10 rule
{{ c.label }} {{ c.hex }}

Every edtech brand reaches for blue and calls it trust. Lavender was already in the product, so I built out from it: teal as secondary, a light orange and champagne as tertiary, grayscale carrying the weight, and enough range for pending, error and success states to live inside the brand instead of being bolted on.

Campaign type

Cabinet Grotesk

Hoardings, covers, campaign headlines. Never product UI.

Product type

Plus Jakarta Sans

Titles, labels, buttons. Inter for every line of body copy.

Wordmark

Lowercase, rounded, lavender. One weight, one colour, one fixed position in every layout.

The mascot

Fixed identity. Flexible expression.

siya isn't another AI assistant, it's a companion, and a companion needs a face. Two anchors never change: the lavender body and the glasses. Without glasses it's a generic blob; with them it reads intelligent, slightly nerdy, and credible in front of a principal.

The CEO and PM laughed at the first version. The PM said it looked like a ghost. I didn't argue, I built mockups until the idea had context: on a hoodie, on a business card, in an empty state, in onboarding, disappointed when a student slips. Seeing it as a set is what turned the room around.

A mascot is a character. A brand is a system.

Eight expressions, one rule set. Glossy lavender, 3D-rendered, never redrawn as flat vector. Minimum 64px, else the wordmark takes over.

✦ 08 · The collateral

The argument I couldn't win in a meeting, I won in mockups.

A mascot on a slide is a drawing. The same mascot on a mug, a hoodie, a sticker sheet, a poster on the office wall and an ID card around a new joiner's neck is a company. So I stopped defending the character and started showing the surfaces.

The internal surfaces

Brand isn't only what customers see.

The ID card was the piece that made the system real inside the company. Two-sided: photo, name and role on the front against a lavender field; emergency contact, a QR to the site, and the mascot in a suit holding a briefcase on the back, the one place the character is allowed to be formal.

The same rules produced the letterhead template and the brochure: one grid, one type pairing, the wordmark in a fixed position, and a strict rule that the mascot never appears on a legal document.

✦ 09 · The social system

The post template the marketing team runs without me.

Social was the surface most likely to drift, it ships fastest and nobody asks a designer first. So instead of designing posts I designed slots: a base template with a dark opener and closer, light cards between them, a fixed logo position, a fixed page counter, one accent word per headline. Marketing fills it; nothing drifts.

Two templates that needed a second pass

Both first versions were structurally fine and unreadable at thumb size. The fix was the same each time: give the reader an entry point before the sentence.

✦ 10 · The presentation module

A slide system for teachers who are not designers.

A template library built long before it was needed, lesson content already lived in the product, so a classroom deck should have been a click away. The hard part was never generation; it was making sure a machine filling these templates could not produce an ugly slide. Fixed left-aligned titles, one idea per slide, image wells that hold their shape empty, bullet lists capped before they overflow, a “continued” state for anything longer.

The player

Built for a projector and one hand.

A thumbnail rail to jump mid-lesson without leaving the deck, a slide counter that stays visible, controls that fade until the cursor moves, and full-screen as the default rather than a setting. Everything a teacher needs while facing away from the laptop.

How it actually ended

I designed the whole module. It didn't ship the way I designed it.

Not every design you're proud of survives contact with a runway. Saying so is part of the job.

Templates, player, empty and continued states, all built and approved in review. Then engineering costed it: a custom renderer that behaved identically on every teacher's laptop and projector was more work than the runway allowed. Reveal.js won.

Right call for the company, wrong outcome for the design. The type scale survived, the layouts mostly didn't, deep customisation is off the table. I documented the gap instead of quietly redesigning around it.

✦ 12 · The migration

A new brand, 100+ screens, and a CEO who wanted it yesterday.

I'd deliberately not built a full design system, because I knew a redesign was coming and didn't want to systemise something we'd throw away. That made the migration expensive: three months by hand. My position was simple, if we redesign it, we redesign it properly.

Stitch, UX Pilot and the first Claude attempts weren't close, and I said so. Months later Claude Design shipped with design systems in the loop, I tested it, and the results changed how we work. My whole workflow moved off Figma: I design in Claude, share the link, developers host from it. Where it can't get there, I pull the HTML into Figma, fix it by hand, and hand that over.
