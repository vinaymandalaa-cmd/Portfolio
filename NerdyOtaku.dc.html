India's first anime embroidery store, as an app.

Nerdy Otaku was a real business started by my seniors: embroidered anime hoodies and sweatshirts, sold mostly through Instagram DMs. I took it as my first serious UX project and asked a harder question than they had. Not “how do we get a website?” but “what would this actually feel like as a product?”

01 / the bet

Why anime merch, in 2022?

For years the received wisdom in India was “anime is for kids”, which is why the shows that landed here were the ones aimed at children. By 2020 that myth was gone: millions were deep in anime and manga, and the entertainment industry still hadn't caught up.

Meanwhile the pandemic had pushed streaming and merch demand through the roof. A market that big with almost no serious homegrown merch experience is not a niche, it's a gap. That's the bet the whole project sits on.

06 / the competition

Three competitors. Nobody had all three things.

I audited the three strongest players in the space. Between them they had good catalogues, decent support, and one had T-shirt customisation. None of them had deep anime range, real customisation and a fast-delivery option in the same product. That absence was the strategy.

08 / the hard one

Embroidery doesn't forgive a free canvas.

Customisation was the most requested feature and the hardest to design honestly. A printed tee can take anything you upload. An embroidered hoodie can't: every extra colour and curve is machine time, and a design that works on screen can be unstitchable.

So the interface tells the truth about the medium. Printable products get full freedom, upload art, add text, place it anywhere. Embroidered products get constrained freedom, curated placements and components instead of a blank canvas. Same builder, two different permission sets.

The loop that closes it

If the user doesn't like what they made, they message support, support passes the intent to the design team, and the design team finishes it. If they do like it, they check out straight from the builder. Every user design then enters a weekly vote, and the winners get shelf space in Best Customized Designs. A customisation feature that produces the catalogue is a feature that pays for itself.

09 / the decision I'm proudest of

I designed live tracking. Then I argued it out.

Interviews asked for it directly: two of five wanted to see the rider on a map. So I built it, and it looked great in the prototype.

My mentor asked one question. Why don't Amazon and Flipkart show you the rider's live location, do you think they can't afford it? Because a rider who's visibly two streets away gets called, chased and pressured, and the customer sits there watching a dot instead of getting an answer.

We rolled it back to status-based tracking with a clear timeline and a text channel to the delivery partner. Same information need. None of the anxiety, none of the surveillance.

10 / the interface

Let the merch be the colour.

Anime artwork is loud by nature, so the UI is deliberately near-monochrome: black, white and four greys, Raleway for text and Poppins for numerals. The product photography and the designs carry all the saturation.

11 / where it ended

The first case study I asked for. Still one of my favourites.

I asked my seniors whether I could build my first proper case study on their brand. They said yes, then went further than that: handed over the brand assets and trusted me with the work.

It stopped being a portfolio exercise somewhere in the middle. It was the first time I carried something from understanding a brand all the way to shaping a product around it, and that was where I fell for the process itself.

The business wound down and the app was never built. The brand is still on Instagram; the product isn't. I'm still proud of it.
